## What this dataset is

64 passages from 40 public web pages in which U.S. colleges, campus offices, campus publications, and platforms talk to students about social media and phones. Each row is one passage, stored as a paraphrase (no direct quotes), with fields recording what the passage says social media is for, who it imagines it is talking to, what evidence it offers, and where it draws the line between productive and distracting use.

The question behind the data: when does advice to students call social media a distraction, and when does it call it a resource? A single "hours used" column cannot hold that difference.

## Source base and how it was built

- Scope: public English-language pages from U.S. colleges and universities, their offices, student newspapers, and two platform feature announcements aimed at college students. Accessed from 2026-10-21 to 2026-11-03. Publication dates range from 2009 to 2026: 18 dated rows are from before 2020, 20 from 2020 to 2021, and 18 from 2024 to 2026, with none from 2022 or 2023.
- Collection: web searches in four deliberate clusters (study advice, counseling and wellbeing, career services, community and belonging), then reading the retrieved text of each page.
- Unit of record: one passage (a recommendation, claim, or example), not one document. Several documents contribute more than one row.
- Privacy: no student names, no usernames, and no long excerpts. Quoted students appear only as "a student" or "a staff member."
- Computation: the dataset was assembled by script, checked with `script.py` (schema, controlled vocabularies, duplicate and quotation checks), and summarized in a cross-tab and chart. Some data that could not be found by the script (e.g. some college names and dates that the articles were published) were manually put in by me.

## From web pages to structured data

**1. Cultural material to working representation.** The cultural material is advice: web pages where colleges, campus offices, student newspapers, and platforms tell students how to think about social media and phones. I read each page and picked out passages that make a recommendation, claim, or example about social media or phone use. Each passage became a short paraphrase plus its citation details. At this stage the page's exact wording, tone, images, layout, and surrounding article are left behind, and my wording stands between the reader and the source, which is why every row keeps its URL.

- Included: public, English-language pages from or about U.S. colleges, or platform features aimed at U.S. college students, that speak to students about social media or phone use.
- Excluded: pages I could not open (blocked or paywalled), pages outside the U.S., and passages that mention social media only in passing without saying how students should use it.

**2. Working representation to structured data.** Each paraphrase became one row in `items.csv`, coded into fixed fields. Four fields (stated_purpose, boundary_framing, evidence_cited, responsibility_frame) use controlled vocabularies so passages can be counted and compared, and the validation script rejects any label outside those lists. Three fields (boundary_rule, imagined_audience, interpretation_note) are free text so they can hold detail the categories flatten. This step forces choices the text doesn't always make: a passage that mixes purposes gets the one it emphasizes most, and every passage must land in distracting, productive, or conditional.

## File format and how to read it

The dataset is `items.csv`, a comma-separated plain-text file (UTF-8, one header row, 64 data rows, one row per passage). I chose CSV because every passage has the same set of fields, so a flat table fits the data, and CSV opens in any programming language or text editor without proprietary software. Source details (title, URL, dates) repeat on every row from the same page, so each row stands on its own; `source_id` links rows from the same page. Text fields containing commas are wrapped in double quotes, following standard CSV rules. Dates use YYYY-MM-DD or `unknown`.

To load it in Python:

```python
import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_csv("items.csv", dtype=str, keep_default_na=False)
```

`dtype=str, keep_default_na=False` reads every value as text, so labels like `none` and `unknown` are not converted to missing data.

To rerun the checks and regenerate the summary table and chart (requires pandas and matplotlib):

```
python script.py items.csv
```

## Data dictionary

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| item_id | Row ID (I001 to I064) |
| source_id | ID of the source page (S01 to S40); rows with the same ID come from the same page |
| institution | Institution or outlet that published the page |
| source_type | academic_support_advice, institutional_news_advice, campus_publication, career_services, counseling_wellness, platform_feature, news_coverage |
| source_title, url, access_date | Citation details; access date is when the page was read |
| publication_date | ISO date, year-month, or `unknown` when the retrieved text did not show it |

| stated_purpose | What the passage says the use is for: study_focus, procrastination, wellbeing, news_following, professional_networking, community_belonging |
| boundary_framing | distracting, productive, or conditional (the passage says it depends) |
| boundary_rule | The condition that decides it (time, intention, platform, content, etc.), or "none stated" |
| imagined_audience | Who the passage appears to be written for |
| evidence_cited | none, anecdote, staff_expertise, interviewee_opinion, statistic_unsourced, statistic_sourced, research_unnamed, research_named |
| responsibility_frame | Who is asked to act: individual, shared, institutional, platform_design, unstated |
| interpretation_note | Short interpretive note |

## Interpretive choices to document

- **"Conditional" exists on purpose.** A binary of helpful versus harmful would have forced passages like "take scheduled social media breaks" or "use it purposefully" into one side. The boundary_rule field records what the condition is.
- **Purpose is taken from the text, not inferred about the writer.** If a passage does not say why someone would use social media, the label follows the context the passage supplies.
- **Platform-level contrasts are kept.** Career staff often separate "LinkedIn is professional" from "Facebook is social," which draws the boundary by platform and not by behavior.
- **Paraphrase only.** Public posts and articles are still sensitive and searchable, so passages are restated and no names are kept.

## Early pattern (descriptive only)

Passages about study focus are overwhelmingly labeled distracting or conditional (24 of 25), while passages about professional networking and community are mostly productive (20 of 26). Productive framings more often point to institutional services (10 of 21), while distracting framings more often put responsibility on the individual (18 of 22). See `chart_framing_by_purpose.png` and `summary_framing_by_purpose.csv`.

These counts describe this sample only. Because the searches were aimed at each kind of advice separately, the share of "distracting" versus "productive" rows reflects the search design and says nothing about how common each framing is. The comparison across purposes is the more meaningful signal.

## Known limitations

- Convenience sample from search results, not a systematic crawl. It over-represents pages that rank well and use common terms.
- 8 of 64 rows have unknown publication dates because no date appeared on the page.
- About a third of dated rows (20 of 56) come from 2020 to 2021, so remote-learning advice is a noticeable part of the sample due to the pandemic at the time. No rows come from 2022 or 2023.
- Student voices appear mainly through the institutions and newspapers that choose to publish them.

## Reuse

Use the data to study how institutions frame student technology use, not as evidence of what students actually do or what affects grades. Check URLs before citing, since pages move and disappear. Do not republish the source pages' text.
