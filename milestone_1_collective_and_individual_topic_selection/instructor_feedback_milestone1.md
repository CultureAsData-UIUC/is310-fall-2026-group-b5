# Instructor Feedback on Milestone 1

## Collective Feedback on Topic, Scholarly Context, and Collaboration Plan

I like your shared interest in academic performance, especially the question of what disappears when student experiences become hours, scores, or GPA. Gaming, social media, mental health, and sleep give you distinct projects that can still speak to each other. The topic feels cohesive across the group, and the scope seems reasonable. I also appreciated that you bounded the topic to U.S. university contexts, though it would be useful to check whether the existing datasets actually represent that population rather than assume they fit the scope.

I think the strongest shared question is how academic success is represented and measured, rather than which personal behavior causes a student's grades. Your scholarship on GPA and grading practices is particularly useful here. You might carry that perspective into the individual projects by asking who defines success, productivity, or distraction and what assumptions become embedded in a field. A small dataset would not establish causation, but it could make those interpretive choices visible.

I really appreciated the attention to how the existing data was constructed. Tergel's discussion of synthetic data and Cici's questions about merged records give you concrete audit directions. It would be useful to document the evidence for those claims while leaving room for the audit to change your initial understanding. The scholarship you identified also gives the group a substantial starting point. As the project documentation develops, you can turn the current links into references and note which ideas are especially useful for shaping each person's fields, methods, or ethical choices.

The most important next step is the custom-data collection plan. You cannot run surveys or collect biological data for this course, and because your final work will be shared publicly, you should not collect classmates' diaries, mental-health information, grades, sleep measurements, or academic records. Removing names would not necessarily make those records safe to publish. Instead, you might consider how your individual datasets can ask cultural questions about academic life through public institutional guidance, campus publications, online discourse, fiction, news coverage, existing research data, or carefully bounded self-tracking. Public materials still need careful contextualization and publication decisions, but they allow you to examine academic culture rather than turn private student experiences into a correlation study.

Your group chat and individual folders are a useful start. Moving forward, I would encourage you to make the division of labor for group assignments more concrete and begin planning the collective website, shared principles, and repository documentation. You might decide where data, code, documentation, and website files will live, who could take responsibility for particular shared tasks, how you might review one another's categories and publication choices, and how group logs and commits could make everyone's contributions visible. I also recommend renaming the milestone folder to a short cross-platform name such as `milestone-1`, since the colon in its current name creates problems for Windows users. You can keep the full milestone title inside the document.

Overall, I think you have a coherent topic, a reasonable shared scope, and promising audit questions. This milestone gives you a strong starting point. For the next stage, you might focus on revising the privacy-sensitive collection plans, clarifying the custom records and computational work, and expanding the collaboration plan. You do not need to discard the shared question; the opportunity is to investigate it responsibly and feasibly through cultural materials rather than surveys or biological measures. Happy to discuss more if you have any questions!

## Individual Feedback

### Tergel

I really like your distinction between how students describe gaming and how the existing dataset models it. You could preserve how gaming is framed as leisure, community, competition, coping, or distraction, including examples that have several meanings. You might use paraphrased observations rather than reproducing full posts, avoid unnecessary identifying information, and discuss questionable cases with me. It would also be useful to describe how you selected the material, since a search or personalized feed is not a representative picture of student experience.

Computation could help organize accessible metadata, identify repeated items, or assist with entering your annotations. It would be worth checking platform access before making scraping central to the plan. You could also compare the fields in your custom records with the synthetic dataset's schema, asking what happens when purposes and experiences become a number such as hours played. The National College Health Assessment material you identified may offer another useful point of comparison because it represents student experience through a different institutional method. It would help to note whether it provides aggregate findings, downloadable data, or scholarly context, since those support different kinds of claims.

For the audit, you might compare the description with any available generation code or documentation and distinguish what you can establish from what remains unknown. How do the imposed assumptions shape the patterns a user might discover, and what becomes visible in the public narratives that the synthetic fields cannot represent? The gaming and student-health scholarship you identified seems like a useful foundation for refining those categories. Overall, I think this is moving in a strong direction if you keep the focus on representation rather than attempt to prove gaming's effect on grades. Happy to discuss next steps if you have questions about narrowing the source base, preserving context, or making publication decisions!

### Cici

I really appreciated your attention to the provenance of the merged dataset. Asking whether a row actually represents one person, rather than just several records with matching IDs, gets directly at the interpretive and technical choices that can disappear inside a finished CSV, which could become a particularly useful auditing approach.

I think it would also be useful to investigate how the merge happened rather than assume the documentation proves that unrelated people were joined. You might inspect the source datasets and any available code, record the evidence for your conclusions, and note what you cannot establish. Python could help you check duplicate identifiers, compare schemas, or trace how selected rows were assembled. A matching ID is something to investigate, not automatically evidence of a shared identity.

Your diary proposal also identifies a meaningful distinction: the same amount of social media use can serve very different purposes. However, collecting routines and academic load from classmates through a survey or diary study would raise privacy and consent concerns, especially when the final dataset is intended for reuse. Please do not recruit participants or collect classmates' data for this assignment. I would encourage you to preserve the question about productive and distracting use through a different source base.

One option would be to create data from public university advice, campus publications, existing research data, or platform features that present social media as productive or distracting. Then you could envision whether a row is a document, a passage, or a recommendation? You could record the stated purpose of use, the audience imagined, the evidence cited, and your interpretation of the boundary between productivity and distraction. That would still capture context that a field called hours used cannot preserve.

Another possible direction would be a carefully bounded self-tracking project inspired by [*Dear Data*](https://www.dear-data.com/theproject/). You could document your own uses of social media and create interpretive categories for purpose, context, or perceived value without asking other students to disclose their routines. That would represent one situated experience rather than undergraduate students generally, so the documentation could make that limit explicit. You could also decide which details you are comfortable publishing rather than assume every observation belongs in the final reusable dataset.

Computation could assist with extracting accessible text, organizing the materials, or building a data-entry tool for your annotations. The connection to the audit could be the categories and assumptions about productive use, rather than matching individual students across the datasets. You might consider how your custom records could question the merged dataset's definitions without claiming both sources describe the same population.

The research you identified seems useful for developing the distinction between productive and distracting use, especially if you let it inform the categories rather than begin with a simple positive-versus-negative binary. I think you have a thoughtful audit already, and a revised custom-data plan could make this a strong project about how student activity becomes classified as distraction. Happy to brainstorm more ideas as you proceed!

### Allison

I really like your distinction between how long students sleep and why their sleep takes a particular form. Your interest in the expectation that students should sacrifice sleep for productivity is a clear cultural question, and it connects well to the group's attention to what numerical measures leave out.

As with Cici's proposal, I would encourage you to reconsider the direct diary collection plan before proceeding. You cannot collect surveys or biological data from participants for this course, and sleep routines linked to academic activity can reveal personal information even without names. You could preserve the same question through public campus discourse about sleep, workloads, employment, or all-nighters rather than collecting classmates' routines.

For your custom dataset, you might select approximately 50-100 passages, advice items, or other public materials within a defined period or institutional setting. Fiction, news coverage, and student newspapers may be especially useful places to examine the idea of a campus “no sleep culture.” For example, the *Daily Bruin* article [“The Quad: Exploring Anti-Sleep Culture, How Students May Be Affected”](https://dailybruin.com/2020/03/12/the-quad-exploring-anti-sleep-culture-how-students-may-be-affected) offers one example of students publicly describing this phenomenon. What does each source present as a reason for losing sleep, and how does it frame health, responsibility, productivity, or success? You could preserve the evidence behind your interpretation, including cases that challenge the assumption that sleep sacrifice is always encouraged, while distinguishing what these sources represent from what students actually experience.

If you already collect sleep information about yourself and prefer a self-tracking approach, you could also design a carefully bounded personal dataset. That would document one situated experience rather than make claims about university students generally. You might decide which information you are comfortable publishing and use the cultural materials to contextualize your observations rather than treat your own measurements as biological evidence about a wider population.

Computation could help collect accessible text, organize sources, or compare your categories with the fields in the audit dataset. A small data-entry tool could support your close annotations, while Python could help inspect missingness or compare schemas in the existing data. It would be useful to consider what those procedures might reveal and what would still depend on interpreting the sources.

For the audit, you might choose one primary source and see whether the underlying records and documentation are accessible and reusable. The capstone analysis page is not necessarily a downloadable dataset. You could investigate how sleep and academic success were measured, what population is represented, and what remains absent. A device-derived estimate, a self-report, and a narrative about tiredness would be different forms of evidence, not interchangeable versions of the same number.

The sleep research you identified seems like a useful foundation for thinking about the relationship among sleep, workload, productivity, and academic success. Your cultural materials might connect to the audit through those concepts or through the reasons given for particular sleep patterns, without representing the same individuals. I think you have a strong cultural phenomenon to explore. A helpful next step might be to decide exactly which records will let you add complexity to “no sleep culture,” alongside a settled audit source. Happy to discuss next steps if you have questions!
